\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Indonesian has roughly 270 million speakers but comparatively thin NLP infrastructure. The clitic \id{-nya} appears in approximately 19.5\% of suffix occurrences in Indonesian corpus data~\cite{denistia2021morphology} and encodes at least four functions: possessive, definite, anaphoric, and nominalising.

Standard Indonesian retrieval pipelines often strip \id{-nya} with stemmers such as Nazief--Adriani~\cite{nazief1996indonesian} or Sastrawi~\cite{sastrawi}. The canonical Indonesian stemming study by Tala~\cite{tala2003stemming} predates dense retrieval, while modern multilingual encoders such as BGE-m3~\cite{chen2024bgem3} and E5~\cite{wang2024multilingual_e5} inherit preprocessing choices that have rarely been controlled-tested for Indonesian. Camacho-Collados et al.~\cite{camacho2023preprocessing} report mixed, task-dependent effects of classical preprocessing on Transformer-based classifiers, often finding no benefit on English-centric benchmarks; \id{-nya} handling is therefore an empirical question rather than a safe default.

A second concern is stripping quality. A naive regex such as \texttt{(\textbackslash w+?)nya\textbackslash b} over-strips non-clitic words including \id{punya}, \id{tanya}, \id{hanya}, \id{Kenya}, \id{Sonya}, and \id{Tanya}. Dictionary-guarded Sastrawi stripping protects many such cases but still depends on lexicon coverage, including for adverbial forms such as \id{biasanya}. We therefore measure both naive harm and whether any \id{-nya}-aware preprocessing helps modern retrievers.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A controlled MIRACL-id study of five \id{-nya} preprocessing strategies for both BM25 and BGE-m3.
    \item Retrieval measurements for naive stripping, dictionary-guarded stripping, sentinel masking, and an in-house rule-based anaphora resolver.
    \item A sensitivity-stratified analysis of where \id{-nya} preprocessing matters most.
\end{itemize}
